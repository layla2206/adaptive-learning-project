# Empathize-Phase Interview Guide
### Adaptive Learning Pace Project — "How might we help students learn at a pace and in a format that matches their individual understanding?"

## Where this sits in the process, and why now

Most design thinking frameworks (the Stanford d.school's five-mode model — Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test — is the most commonly taught version, though I don't have a single canonical citation for it) put Empathize *before* Define. Your team wrote the HMW and built out the problem framing, prioritization matrix, and scoring criteria first — which is a completely normal way for a course project to unfold, but it means the HMW so far is a team hypothesis, not yet grounded in first-hand observation. The interview round is how you go back and pressure-test that hypothesis.

Concretely, this round should give you two things:

1. **Validated or revised problem framing** for slides 2–4 — real language and real moments to replace (or confirm) the qualitative claims already there.
2. **Movement on the four blockers on slide 6** — interviews are especially useful for narrowing "who is the target institution/user base," and can surface hints about data availability and current tools/process (feeding "Data" and "Process" scoreability), even though they won't settle "AI model architecture" or "infrastructure plan" on their own — those will likely need a separate technical scoping conversation.

## Who to talk to

Aim for two segments, since the deck already claims impact on both:

- **Students** — deliberately mix ability levels and self-reported pace (some who feel behind, some who feel held back, some in the middle), and mix subjects/formats if you can (lecture-based, self-paced online, lab/practical). This range matters more than raw count.
- **Instructors/TAs** — people currently trying (or failing to find time) to differentiate pace or feedback for individual students.

A common qualitative-research rule of thumb is that 5–8 interviews per segment starts surfacing repeated themes, but that's a guideline, not a hard rule — check your course's own requirements, since "enough" for a class deliverable may be different from "enough" for genuine saturation.

Team-of-5 logistics: e.g., 2 people interview students, 2 interview instructors, 1 floats/takes notes and preps synthesis — rotate so everyone gets interviewing reps.

## Interview structure (about 20–30 min each)

1. **Warm-up (2–3 min)** — low-stakes, builds rapport, gets them talking before you ask anything sensitive.
2. **Story-invoking questions (bulk of the interview)** — ask about *specific past moments*, not opinions or hypotheticals. Opinions tell you what people think they think; stories tell you what actually happened.
3. **Laddering ("why" follow-ups)** — after any interesting answer, ask "what made that hard/good?" or "why do you think that happened?" two or three times to get past the surface answer.
4. **Closing (2–3 min)** — wrap-up, ask if there's anything you didn't ask about that they think matters, thank them.

Etiquette:
- Avoid yes/no and leading questions ("Don't you wish your class moved slower?" is leading — "Tell me about a time the pace of a class felt wrong" is not).
- Let silence sit — people often add the most honest detail after a pause.
- Capture exact phrases/quotes, not paraphrases — these are gold for slide justification later and for empathy maps.
- Don't pitch your AI concept during the interview. You're gathering, not selling — introducing the idea early will bias what they tell you.

## Question bank

### Warm-up
- Can you walk me through what a typical week of classes/coursework looks like for you right now?
- What subject or class do you feel most confident in, and what does that experience feel like?

### Story-invoking: pace & pacing struggles
- Tell me about a time you felt completely lost during a lesson or lecture — what happened right before that moment?
- Tell me about a time a class moved too slowly for you — what did you end up doing?
- Walk me through the last time you had to re-learn something on your own because the class pace didn't work for you. Where did you go, and why there?
- Describe a moment when you realized you understood something before everyone else in the room. What did you do next?

### Story-invoking: feedback moments
- Tell me about the last piece of feedback you got on your work that actually changed how you approached the next assignment.
- Describe a time feedback came too late to be useful. What would "in time" have looked like?
- Walk me through what happens between you submitting work and finding out whether you actually understood it.

### Story-invoking: format preferences
- Tell me about a time you learned something well outside of class — what format was it in, and what made it click?
- Describe the last time you switched from one way of learning something (video, reading, practice problems, asking someone) to another mid-task. What triggered the switch?

### Instructor/TA segment
- Tell me about the last time you tried to give a struggling student extra help while the rest of the class kept moving. What did that actually look like logistically?
- Walk me through how you currently figure out which students are lost versus which are bored, in a given week.
- Describe a specific week where you felt stretched thin trying to meet different students where they were. What took up the most time?
- Tell me about a tool or workaround you've tried to personalize pace or feedback. What happened when you tried to scale it beyond one or two students?

### Closing
- If you could change one thing about how pace or feedback works in your classes, what would it be?
- Is there anything about how you learn (or teach) that we haven't asked about but that you think matters here?

## After the interviews: synthesis

1. **Affinity mapping** — pull every notable quote/observation onto its own sticky note (physical or digital, e.g. FigJam/Miro), cluster by theme as a team.
2. **Empathy map or short persona sketch per segment** — what they say / do / think / feel, built only from what you actually heard, not assumptions.
3. **Insight statements** — turn clusters into "[user] needs [need] because [surprising reason]" statements. These are what you should quote or reference back on slides 3–4 in place of the currently qualitative-only claims.
4. **Feed back into slide 6** — specifically use instructor answers to sanity-check the "Process" and "People" feasibility categories, and use both segments' answers on tools/platforms already in use to inform "Data" and "Platform." Target institution/user base (one of the four blockers) should get noticeably more concrete once you see who actually showed up with the sharpest pain points.

## A caution on this guide itself

This is a synthesis of commonly taught qualitative-research/design-thinking interviewing practice, not a citation of a specific paper or course requirement — if your instructor has specified a required format (e.g., a particular number of interviews, a specific synthesis template), follow that over this guide.
